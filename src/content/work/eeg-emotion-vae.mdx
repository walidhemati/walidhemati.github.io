## Problem

<p class="lede">The brain signal is rich; the label is one word.</p>

An EEG recording is many channels sampled many times a second, full of artefacts, and shaped by whoever happens to be wearing the cap. The emotion it is supposed to reveal is a single, coarse label. Training a classifier straight from raw signal to label means learning everything at once from very little supervision.

The project asks a narrower question first: can a model learn a compact description of the signal, and does that description carry information about emotion?

## Constraints

- **Public data.** Two established datasets: DEAP, EEG recorded while participants watched music videos and rated their own reactions, and SEED, EEG recorded while participants watched film clips chosen to be positive, neutral or negative.
- **Two very different recordings.** DEAP: 32 subjects, 32 channels at 128 Hz, 40 one-minute trials each, rated for valence and arousal (above 5 counts as positive). SEED: 15 subjects, 62 channels at 200 Hz, film clips labelled positive, neutral or negative; we dropped the neutral trials.
- **Two toolchains.** EEG work lives in MATLAB as much as in Python, so the code uses both. It started from the public code of Li et al.'s latent-factor-decoding work and extends it to SEED and to GRU models.

A variational autoencoder has two halves. The encoder maps a recording to a distribution over a few latent factors; the decoder has to rebuild the signal from a sample of them. Because everything must pass through that narrow space, the factors are pushed to capture what is consistent in the signal rather than its noise. Emotion detection then works on those factors instead of on the raw channels.

In practice the signals are z-scored per subject, and the encoder (two dense layers, built in Keras) maps each time sample's channel vector to up to 16 latent factors. A recurrent network, an LSTM or a GRU with 200 units, then reads a trial as a sequence of factor vectors, one every three seconds, and outputs positive or negative. The same pipeline runs with four other encoders for comparison: PCA, ICA, a restricted Boltzmann machine (as a deep belief network) and a plain autoencoder.

## Results

**Reconstruction.** With 16 latent factors the VAE rebuilds the signal at a mean Pearson correlation of 0.99 on DEAP and 0.95 on SEED. The RBM, the autoencoder and PCA get just as close, and ICA stays far below the others. Most of the gain comes from the first few factors.

**Emotion.** Rebuilding the signal well is not the same as keeping the emotion in it. That is why every encoder's factors also went through the recurrent classifiers, scored by F1 on a 30 % held-out split.

<Results metrics={frontmatter.metrics}>
  On DEAP the VAE factors with an LSTM were the clear winner: F1 0.877 for arousal and 0.834 for valence, where every other encoder stayed between 0.50 and 0.64. The picture is not uniform, though. With a GRU instead of an LSTM, the VAE's lead on DEAP disappears (arousal 0.603, the lowest of the five). On SEED, PCA matches or slightly beats it (0.898 vs 0.885 with an LSTM, 0.901 vs 0.896 with a GRU). The full per-subject tables are in the [paper](https://github.com/walidhemati/Biosignals-Based-Latent-Factor-Decoding-for-emotion-detection-using-a-Variational-Autoencoder/blob/main/Biosignals_Based_Latent_Factor_Decoding_for_emotion_detection_using_a_Variational_Autoencoder%20(1).pdf).

## What I'd do next

- Evaluate subject-independently. The 70/30 split pools trials from all subjects, so a subject's data can sit on both sides of it; leave-one-subject-out is the stricter test.
- Find out why the VAE's lead on DEAP holds with an LSTM but not with a GRU.
- Give the repository a top-level README and relative paths, so the code runs without the paper open next to it.
